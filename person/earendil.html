etymology

  • Eärendil was his given father-name. Eärendil is a Quenya name, meaning "Devoted to the Sea", literally "Sea lover". It is a compound of eär and -ndil. Tolkien used the original Old English name Eärendel for his early Legendarium, which he envisioned as Elvish, derived form the Qenya words ea ("eagle") and earen ("eyre"); the exact meaning of the name remained unclear.

other names

  • The Sindarin cognate for Eärendil was Gaerdil, with variations like Gaerdilion and Gaerennil. There are also experimental Sindarized forms, such as Aerendil and Aerennel, as well as a direct translation: Seron Aearon. His mother-name was Ardamírë, a prophetic name, as it means "Jewel of the World", from arda ("world") + mírë ("jewel"). Its Sindarin version was Mír n'Ardhon. Eärendil was translated as Azrubêl in Adûnaic, from azar ("sea") and the stem bel-. Eärendil was known by many epithets: Half-elven, the Blessed, Bright, and the Mariner.

other versions

  • Before conceiving any story for his mythology, Tolkien wrote some poems that dealt with Eärendel. The earliest was Éalá Éarendel Engla Beorhtast, which would set the foundations for The Book of Lost Tales as Tolkien "tried to find out" what it was about. The other poems were The Bidding of the Minstrel, The Shores of Faëry and The Happy Mariners, all written between 1914-1915. The four poems tell in a lyric way about the coming of Eärendel to Valinor, the rising of his boat as the morning star and his everlasting travel in the edge of the world.

    Cited to: J.R.R. Tolkien, Christopher Tolkien (ed.), The Book of Lost Tales Part Two, "V. The Tale of Eärendel", p. 267–273

    [EXT] C? Tolkien Gateway, “Eärendil”, revid 438062 (2026-04-27T09:08:48Z)

  • The Bidding of the Minstrel is associated with a noted page, which outlines the travels of Eärendel to the North, visiting Island, Iceland, magic lands and seeing Kôr from afar; then he goes South, travelling through the Mediterranean or Atlantic sea, full of strange creatures. Then he travels to the western lip of the world and sails upon the sky, never coming back. The Shores of Faëry has a short preface in prose, which, as in the former text, conceives Eärendel as an old man when travelling to the firmament.

    Cited to: J.R.R. Tolkien, Christopher Tolkien (ed.), The Book of Lost Tales Part Two, "V. The Tale of Eärendel", p. 261–262
