etymology

  • Vána is Quenya for "beautiful", from root BAN, related to unmarred beauty. She was also called the Ever-young.

    Cited to: J.R.R. Tolkien, "Words, Phrases and Passages in Various Tongues in The Lord of the Rings: Eldarin Roots and Stems", in Parma Eldalamberon XVII (edited by Christopher Gilson), p. 150; J.R.R. Tolkien, Christopher Tolkien (ed.), The Silmarillion, "Valaquenta: Of the Valar"

    [EXT] C? Tolkien Gateway, “Vána”, revid 414066 (2024-12-04T21:37:10Z)

history

  • Like her sister, Vána had influence with the flora and fauna of Middle-earth, "all flowers spring as she passes and open if she glances upon them; and all birds sing at her coming." She had "the beauty of both heaven and earth upon her face and in all her works."

    Cited to: J.R.R. Tolkien, Christopher Tolkien (ed.), The Silmarillion, "Valaquenta: Of the Valar"; J.R.R. Tolkien, Christopher Tolkien (ed.), Morgoth's Ring, "Part Three. The Later Quenta Silmarillion: (I) The First Phase: 1. Of the Valar"

    [EXT] C? Tolkien Gateway, “Vána”, revid 414066 (2024-12-04T21:37:10Z)

  • Nessa, the sister of Vána's spouse Oromë, wedded Tulkas on the Isle of Almaren, the Valar's first dwelling. Vána robed Nessa with her flowers for the wedding.

    Cited to: J.R.R. Tolkien, Christopher Tolkien (ed.), Morgoth's Ring, "Part Two. The Annals of Aman"

    [EXT] C? Tolkien Gateway, “Vána”, revid 414066 (2024-12-04T21:37:10Z)

  • Vána dwelt in gardens filled with golden flowers and often came to the forests of Oromë. In the days of the Two Trees of Valinor, the Maia maiden, Arien, "tended to the golden flowers of the gardens of Vána by watering them with the bright dews from Laurelin". Melian was another Maia who initially served Vána and Estë before she departed to Middle-earth.

    Cited to: J.R.R. Tolkien, Christopher Tolkien (ed.), The Silmarillion, "Quenta Silmarillion: Of the Sun and Moon and the Hiding of Valinor"; J.R.R. Tolkien, Christopher Tolkien (ed.), The Silmarillion, "Valaquenta: Of the Maiar"

    [EXT] C? Tolkien Gateway, “Vána”, revid 414066 (2024-12-04T21:37:10Z)

other names

  • In The Book of Lost Tales she is also referred to in full as Vána Tuivána.

    Cited to: J.R.R. Tolkien, Christopher Tolkien (ed.), The Book of Lost Tales Part One, "III. The Coming of the Valar and the Building of Valinor", p. 84

    [EXT] C? Tolkien Gateway, “Vána”, revid 414066 (2024-12-04T21:37:10Z)

other versions

  • In the earliest form of the legendarium in The Book of Lost Tales, she and Oromë had a daughter, Nielíqui. Whilst in the origins of the story of the Two Trees, Vána played a formative role in the growth of Laurelin

    Cited to: J.R.R. Tolkien, Christopher Tolkien (ed.), The Book of Lost Tales Part One, Index, entry "Nielíqui"; J.R.R. Tolkien, Christopher Tolkien (ed.), The Book of Lost Tales Part One, "III. The Coming of the Valar and the Building of Valinor", p. 71

    [EXT] C? Tolkien Gateway, “Vána”, revid 414066 (2024-12-04T21:37:10Z)

  • Later, when the power of Palúrien had failed to heal the wounds of the Two Trees, Vána's love for Laurelin was so great that it caused the tree's remaining life to come forth one last time as a fruit of gold from which the Valar later fashioned the Sun. Vána's maiden, Urwen, would steer the Sun's vessel across the sky. Vána, who repented of speaking against the harvest of Laurelin's last fruit, cut her hair short to weave the tresses as the sails for the Sun-ship. Among other names, the Sun was called "the Lamp of Vána" in memory of her tears and the hair she offered.

    Cited to: J.R.R. Tolkien, Christopher Tolkien (ed.), The Book of Lost Tales Part One, "VIII. The Tale of the Sun and Moon", p. 186

    [EXT] C? Tolkien Gateway, “Vána”, revid 414066 (2024-12-04T21:37:10Z)

  • After the Darkening of Valinor and the flight of the Noldoli to the Great Lands, most of the Valar were glad to have their ancient peace back, wishing neither the rumours of Melko and his violence nor the murmur of the restless Noldoli to disturb them again. For such reasons, they sought the concealment and protection of their land Aman. It was said that particularly Vána and Nessa were of one mind in this matter, in accordance with most of the other Valar.

    Cited to: J.R.R. Tolkien, Christopher Tolkien (ed.), The Book of Lost Tales Part One, "IX. The Hiding of Valinor", pp. 208-209
