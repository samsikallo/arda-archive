etymology

  • Fëanor is an unusual combination of Quenya and Sindarin: fëa ("spirit, soul") + naur ("fire"). The name is actually a half Sindarized version of his Quenya mother-name Fëanáro; the proper Sindarin form should have been Faenor. The name has changed since early versions of the legendarium. In the Gnomish Lexicon, written around 1917, Fëanor is given as the Qenya cognate of Gnomish Fionor ("Goblet Smith"), but is mentioned with no apparent connection to the later Noldoli character. However, Tolkien used Fëanor in the narrative of the Lost Tales for the character. In the Noldorin phase, Fëanor is the original name while Fëanáro is the Qenya cognate. Both meant "Radiant Sun", from Primitive Quendian Phayanâro.

history

  • The Valar realized that Melkor was behind Fëanor's actions. They sent Tulkas to imprison him again, but he could not be found. Melkor was not seen for a long time until, in , he unexpectedly showed up at Formenos. Melkor tried again to manipulate Fëanor, but he went too far, and Fëanor realized that Melkor's true goal was to obtain the Silmarils. He shut the door in Melkor's face and, in a rage, Melkor left. When the Valar heard of the incident, they resumed their hunt, but it was apparent that Melkor had fled from Valinor.

other names

  • When Fëanor was born, he was first called Minyon ("First-begotten"). His father-name was at first the same as his father: Finwë, or simply Finwion ("Son of Finwë"). But when he showed his abilities it was enlarged to Curufinwë, by adding curu ("skill"). His mother-name was Fëanáro, which translates as "Spirit of Fire", being formed by adding fëa ("spirit") and nár ("flame"). Fëanor called himself the "Son of the Þerindë" after the shifting of “Þ" to "s". In the days of the Flight of the Noldor, Fëanor believed that the Silmarils had become symbols and instruments of his power, and thus bestowed upon himself the title of the "lord of the lights".

other versions

  • The character of Fëanor first appears in the Book of Lost Tales, in which Fëanor, one of the Noldoli, gathers pearls and other jewels, and stores in them the light of the trees Laurelin and Silpion, creating the three Silmarils: gems that shine with their own light, and surpass all other jewels in beauty. When Melko begins to spread discontent among the Noldoli, Fëanor, along with Inwë and Ellu Melemno, counsel Nólemë that Manwë should be informed of Melko's doings. However, Melko also speaks to Manwë, telling him that the Noldoli are stirring against the Valar, and when Nólemë's embassy arrives, Manwë is displeased with both parties, and sends Melko back to Mandos, and orders that the Noldoli leave their home upon Kôr and dwell elsewhere, lest their discontent spread among the other kindreds.

  • The Noldoli relocate to the vale of Sirnúmen and remain there for a while, until the time of a great feast held in Valmar, to which they fare along with the rest of the Eldar. During the feast, Melko and his accomplices travel to the emptied dwelling of the Noldoli and slay the guards they had left there, including Fëanor's father, Bruithwir. They plunder the treasury there, taking the most glorious gems, including the Silmarils. After this tragedy, the Noldoli are distraught, and Manwë grants them permission to return to their home at Kôr. However, some, Fëanor among them, choose to remain behind in Sirnúmen. There Fëanor broods, striving and failing to recreate the Silmarils, and sitting beside his father's tomb, his discontent growing until at last he fares to Kôr, speaking against the Valar and urging the Noldoli to leave Valinor. Soon afterwards, following Melko and Gwerlum's destruction of the Two Trees, Fëanor persuades his people to arm themselves, follow him and leave Valinor.

  • According to earlier outlines of The Book of Lost Tales, the Noldoli spread out in the land of Hisilómë. Different outlines from around this time have different fates of Fëanor. In one, he is lost at the Waters of Asgon, sailing out to the Islanded Rock there in search of his jewels. In another, it is Nólemë that dies at Asgon, and Fëanor, rather than his son (as in later versions, and the other outline), leads a host against Melko, and is captured and tormented. In later outlines, the Noldoli also reach Hisilómë, and Fëanor drowns in the Waters of Mithrim. In this version, Maidros, son of Fëanor, is the one to lead a host against Melko and be captured. While Fëanor's manner of death here is different from that in the published Silmarillion, it mirrors it in that he dies soon after leading the Noldoli out of Middle-earth, and is survived by his sons and his people.
