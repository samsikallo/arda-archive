Of the silences

Where the corpus is silent, the archive says so exactly.

An archive that hides its gaps cannot be checked, and an unfalsifiable archive is a story. So the blank space on these leaves is ruled rather than filled, and every silence carries the name of whatever looked for the thing and did not find it.

A silence may only be asserted by something that looked. That rule was written after a fall-through branch in a generator published two hundred and eight claims that the corpus did not say what contained a place — a sentence about Tolkien reached by not matching three tests about the data.

It widened within the hour: a hand may not assert a silence it did not search either. A fall-through has a shape and can be swept for. A sentence typed by a careful reader has no shape at all, and looks exactly like a measured silence.

The cost of not looking is not only a false claim. It is a citation the archive owned and could not see. Of four false silences found in one week, one upgraded a claim: the placing of Taniquetil, recorded as an inference about where the Vanyar dwelt, is stated outright in the Silmarillion twice.

So a silence audit is not only a hunt for overclaiming. It recovers evidence.

The rule as it stands

Never write that the corpus does not say X without having asked ardatext for X — and without asking whether a neighbouring record already says it. [C]
